## PART 1: WHAT THE DATA SAYS IS ACTUALLY WRONG WITH THE REQUEST

The request was "increase speed, agility and stamina as fast as possible." Four problems with that, each with a fix.

### Problem 1: The calendar is the bottleneck this fall, not the training

NCAA legislation adopted 29 June 2026 and effective 1 August 2026 replaced men's lacrosse's fixed September 1 contact date with a Labor Day anchored date. For the class entering junior year in fall 2026, first contact was **7:00 a.m. ET on Wednesday 9 September 2026**. That was six days ago. Off campus contact, phone calls in either direction, recruiting materials, electronic correspondence and official visits all became legal that morning.

Coaches evaluate through the club circuit first, then film, then references from club and high school coaches. Meaningful change in sprint speed, braking capacity or relative strength takes 12 to 20 weeks. Nothing trained between now and November changes a coach's evaluation this fall.

**What this means:** this plan's payoff date is the **summer 2027 club circuit**, which is where most D1 defensive spots for his class actually get decided. If the training runs and the film and outreach do not, he does everything right physically and still does not get seen. Those are two separate projects and both start this week. See Part 6.

### Problem 2: He is chasing the quality that provably does not separate D1 players

Sell et al. (*Journal of Strength & Conditioning Research*, 2018) tested 41 NCAA Division I men's lacrosse players on aerobic fitness (1.5 mile run), 1RM bench, squat and hang clean, grip strength, vertical jump, 3-cone, pro agility, body composition, and 20 and 40 yard sprints.

Starters beat non-starters on **3-cone, 20 yard sprint, 40 yard sprint and vertical jump**. Aerobic fitness did not separate them. The authors concluded anaerobic qualities are the better predictor of starting status.

GPS data across NCAA men's lacrosse seasons separates it further by position. **Midfielders** post the highest sprint distances, top speeds and high speed running distances. **Defenders post the greatest number of decelerations**, and reach higher top speeds and more accelerations than attackers. Most lacrosse sprints happen over 5 to 20 yards.

**What this means:** "stamina" is last on the list of three, by evidence. Top end speed is a midfielder's currency. A pole's currency is braking, re-accelerating and holding an angle inside a 5 to 15 yard box. The plan is weighted accordingly: braking and short acceleration first, max velocity second as a means to raise the ceiling, aerobic capacity third and mostly as a recovery tool.

### Problem 3: He is already the physical outlier, and that is fragile

The average D1 men's lacrosse defenseman is **6'0.6" and 190.7 lb**. He is 6'5" and 226. That is roughly five inches and 35 pounds past the positional mean, and programs including Vermont, Dartmouth and St. Joseph's have made explicit recruiting decisions to target height at close defense.

His size is the recruiting case. He does not need to become a different athlete. He needs to not lose the asset.

NCAA men's lacrosse injury surveillance puts the overall rate at 4.90 to 5.29 per 1000 athlete exposures. The most reported injuries are **concussion (8.0%), ankle sprain (7.7%) and hamstring tear (6.9%)**. 58.3% of injuries are lower extremity. 40.0% of practice injuries are non-contact, meaning trainable.

**What this means:** durability is not a side item in this plan, it is a primary objective. An ankle or a hamstring in March costs him the junior film season and the summer circuit in one shot, and there is no second recruiting year.

### Problem 4: Long levers make deceleration both his biggest gap and his biggest risk

Longer limbs carry a greater moment of inertia, so they accelerate less for a given hip torque. A taller athlete needs a longer, more patient acceleration and a more upright spine out of a start. He also has more momentum to kill on every stop, and at 226 lb that is a large number.

Up to 70% of non-contact ACL injuries occur during poor deceleration rather than jumping or sprinting. The soleus is the main decelerating force at midstance and contributes more than half the vertical support of the centre of mass during running.

**What this means:** the exact quality that gets a 6'5" pole beaten by a 5'10" attackman off the dodge is the same quality that protects his knee. One training emphasis solves both. It gets the largest share of the week.

### Problem 5 (bonus): Night lifting is placed wrong for a speed priority

Flying sprints must be run fresh, early in a session, and specifically not after fatiguing strength or endurance work. Same day concurrent training shows an effect size of about **-0.66 for strength loss versus -0.10 when sessions are separated**, and running interferes more than cycling.

Monday and Wednesday night lifts mean Tuesday and Thursday mornings are the compromised windows, not the fresh ones. The common mistake is to sprint Tuesday morning off a Monday night lift.

**The fix:** run speed work **after school on Monday and Wednesday, before the night lift**, roughly a three hour gap. That stacks the hard CNS work onto the days that are already hard and leaves Thursday and Saturday genuinely easy. This is Charlie Francis high/low structure and it is the single biggest structural improvement available given his fixed schedule. (With Tuesday night club practice, Tuesday is a loaded day and Saturday takes the low-day slot. See Part 2.)

### Why max velocity moved to Monday

Originally Monday was acceleration and Wednesday was max velocity. Tuesday night club
practice makes that dangerous: Wednesday max velocity would put his fastest running of
the week roughly 18 hours after a practice.

Peak hamstring strain occurs in late swing at top speed, not in the first ten yards.
About 60% of hamstring injuries happen during high-speed running, hamstring tear is the
third most reported injury in NCAA men's lacrosse, and sprinting at 95%+ on residual
fatigue is precisely the mechanism.

So Monday, the freshest day of the week coming off Sunday off, takes max velocity.
Wednesday takes acceleration and heavy sled, which tolerate residual fatigue because he
never reaches the hip angles where hamstrings tear.

### The cost, stated plainly

Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday are now three loaded days in a row. That is the price of a
fixed Tuesday night practice and no arrangement avoids it. It is managed by keeping
Tuesday afternoon completely empty, Thursday and Saturday genuinely easy, and Sunday
fully off.

Three high days plus a club practice. Nothing else goes in. Research on weekly sprint
volume shows a U-shaped injury curve: lowest incidence between 5 and 11 maximal velocity
reps per week, with 6 to 10 exposures above 95% of max speed optimal. Eccentric hamstring
strength measurably decreased when players completed 7 to 8 weekly efforts above 90% but
not below 6. More is not available as a lever here. Extra appetite goes into stickwork,
film and sleep.

**Max velocity rep budget: 6 to 8 per week. Count them. Do not exceed 8.**

### Monday monitoring block — 5 minutes, before the warm-up

Body weight, countermovement jump (3 jumps, best of), 10 yd from a 2-point staggered standing start
(2 reps, best of). Same scale, surface, shoes and order every week.

**Readiness rule:** if the vertical is more than 5% below his four-week rolling average,
drop the last exercise from that day's speed session and cut one set from the lift. Two
weeks running means take an unplanned deload. This is the scaled-down version of what
Ballou and Rhea run at Alabama and it is the cheapest injury insurance available.

### If the Friday conditioning hour is not his to control

If Friday is a team session he must do as prescribed, treat it as spent and do **not**
add the Friday content below. Instead move the deceleration and change of direction block
to Wednesday after school, cut Wednesday to three sled sprints, and keep Thursday and
Saturday low. The plan still works. It loses about 15% of its value.

### Standard warm-up (12 minutes, every session)

1. Ankle rocks 10/side, 90/90 hip switches x10, world's greatest stretch x5/side

5. Build-ups 3x20 yd at 60%, 70%, 80%

### SPEED B — Wednesday after school (acceleration emphasis, ~50 min)

> Moved to Wednesday when Tuesday night club was added. Acceleration tolerates residual
> fatigue; max velocity does not.

This is the session for the first three steps out of a break down. For a 6'5" athlete the coaching point is a **longer, more patient acceleration with a more upright spine** than a shorter athlete uses. He should not try to stay low the way a 5'9" midfielder does. He will trip over his own shins.

| | Weeks 1-3 | Weeks 5-7 | Weeks 9-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falling start | 10 yd x4 | 10 yd x3 | — |
| **Break-down start** | 15 yd x3 | 20 yd x3 | 20 yd x3, reactive |
| Heavy sled | 15 yd x4 @ 70% BM | 20 yd x4 @ 85% BM | contrast, see below |
| Rest between all reps | 2-3 min | 3 min | 3 min |

**Break-down start:** he sets up in an actual defensive breakdown, feet chopping, stick out, and sprints on a partner's signal or a dropped ball. Reactive from week 9. Backpedal-to-sprint rotates in from week 5.

> **Why not a 3-point start.** It was in the first draft and it was wrong. A pole never
> starts from a three-point stance; his real entries are a break-down, a backpedal or a
> shuffle. Even in football the 3-point is a lineman and fullback stance, while linebackers
> and defensive backs use a two-point. The recommended progression runs two-point ->
> three-point -> four-point -> blocks, with the two-point first because it most closely
> mimics how acceleration starts in other sports. At 6'5" the deep crouch is also the
> hardest version of a skill he does not need, and the falling start already teaches
> forward shin angle better with no stance skill at all. Start position changes the timed
> number, so this had to change in week 1 or not at all. Corrected 16 Sep 2026.

**Sled loading is deliberately heavy.** Cross et al. found the load maximising power during sled resisted sprinting sits at roughly a 50% decrement of maximum unresisted velocity, which lands at **69 to 96% of body mass**, far above the conventional 7 to 20% guidance. For him that is roughly 155 to 215 lb of sled plus load, adjusted for surface friction. Turf and grass differ a lot, so set it by feel: he should be visibly slow but still running, not marching.

**Weeks 9-11 contrast pairs:** heavy sled 15 yd, rest 90 seconds, free sprint 20 yd. Four pairs. This is the potentiation block.

**Week 4, 8: deload.** Cut reps by 40%, keep intensity.

### SPEED A — Monday after school (max velocity emphasis, ~55 min)

> Moved to Monday, the freshest day of the week coming off Sunday off. Precede it with the
> 5-minute monitoring block: bodyweight, CMJ x3, 10 yd x2.

Run first, fresh, before the night lift. Flying sprints go early in a session when the athlete can produce maximal effort and are avoided after fatiguing strength or endurance work.

The reason a close defenseman trains max velocity at all is **speed reserve**. If game speed is 10 to 15 mph, a 20 mph athlete is working at a lower percentage of his ceiling than an 18 mph athlete doing the same job, so he recovers faster between efforts and holds form later in a game. It is not because he will run 40 yards flat out. It is also the most potent hamstring protection available: exposure above 95% of max sprint speed, even once per week, lowers hamstring injury risk, with 6 to 10 exposures optimal.

| | Weeks 1-3 | Weeks 5-7 | Weeks 9-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wickets (6 ft spacing, 20-25 yd build-in) | x4 runs | x3 runs | — |
| Flying sprint | Fly 10 x4 | Fly 15 x4 | Fly 20 x4 |
| Assisted / overspeed | — | — | 2-3% downhill or light band, x3 |
| Rest between flying reps | 4 min | 4-5 min | 5 min |
| **Max velocity exposures** | **8** | **7** | **7** |

**Wickets do the coaching.** Set at six feet apart with a 20 to 25 yard build-in, reaching 90% or better through them. An athlete who overstrides hits them; an athlete who shuffles runs out of room. This is the cheapest way to fix top speed mechanics without verbal instruction, and it matters more for a tall athlete than a short one because his failure mode is overstriding and reaching.

**On overspeed:** Matt Rhea's Alabama and Indiana model programmed assisted sprinting in progressions of 5, 10, 15 and 20% overspeed. At Indiana, players' top running speed rose by an average of over 3 mph in a year and the roster went from six players capable of 21 mph to 34. **Cap him at 5 to 10%.** The full progression is for a monitored college program with force plates and weekly laser timing. A high school junior on a hill without that oversight should stay at the conservative end.

**Week 4, 8: deload.** Three flying runs, no wickets, no assisted work.

### FRIDAY CONDITIONING HOUR (the highest value hour in the week)

This is where a close defenseman is actually built. Sixty minutes, in this order.

**0-12 min: standard warm-up**

**12-30 min: deceleration and change of direction block**

Full recovery between reps. This is quality work, not conditioning. If he is gassed, he is training the wrong thing and rehearsing bad braking mechanics.

1. **Deceleration ladder.** Sprint 15 yd, stop in three steps, hold the position two seconds. x4. Progress to 20 yd in weeks 5-8, 25 yd in weeks 9-12.
2. **Lateral shuffle to sprint.** 5 yd shuffle, open the hip, 10 yd sprint. x4 per side.
3. **Zig-zag approach drill.** Five cones five yards apart in a zig-zag. Approach with a break down of the feet, take the angle, shuffle hard to the next. x4. This is the closest thing in the plan to the actual job.
4. **Chop step / drop step progression.** Short chop into short drop, medium chop into medium drop, then mixed. x6 total. Add a stick check on the approach from week 5.
5. **Drop step and recover.** Sprint 10 yd, drop step, 10 yd recover. x4 per side.
6. **Reactive 1v1 mirror** with a partner, 6 second reps. x6. Weeks 5-12 only, once the mechanics above are clean.

Build in this order across the plan: sagittal plane first (forward and reverse), then frontal (lateral), then transverse (rotational). Sagittal and frontal develop more easily and should come first.

**30-50 min: repeat sprint block**

Protocol: **6 x 40 yd shuttle (20 yd out, 180 degree turn, 20 yd back) with 25 seconds passive recovery between sprints.** This is the validated repeated shuttle sprint protocol from the field sport literature and it maps almost exactly onto what a pole does in a defensive possession.

| | Weeks 1-3 | Weeks 5-7 | Weeks 9-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sets of 6 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| Rest between sets | 4 min | 3.5 min | 3 min |

**50-60 min: extensive tempo cooldown and prehab**
- 4 x 60 yd at roughly 70% effort, walk back recovery. Charlie Francis used 70% tempo to drive aerobic adaptation and flush the system while supporting CNS recovery rather than competing with it. At 70% it is recovery work, not conditioning. If it feels hard he is running it too fast.
- Copenhagen adduction (see dosing below)
- Seated calf raise 2x15

## PART 4: THE LIFTS

The Monday and Wednesday night sessions are fixed, so this is what they should contain. If he is on a team program he cannot change, add the prehab block to the low days and leave the rest alone. The prehab is non-negotiable; the rest is optimisation.

**Volume rule:** keep work at or above 80% of 1RM to roughly **30% of total volume**. Rhea and Ballou run Alabama and Indiana that way, prioritising power output over weight on the bar. For a long limbed 16 to 17 year old carrying a recruiting year, that is the right bias.

**Why trap bar and not back squat.** At 6'5" the back squat will always under-represent him, because the lever lengths punish him and the limiting factor becomes trunk position rather than leg force. The trap bar puts the load closer to his centre of mass. Bilateral training is also what provides the force foundation for sprinting and jumping, per the unilateral-versus-bilateral meta-analysis, so this is the lift that drives his speed. Test and build the trap bar. Keep the squat as an accessory if he likes it.

> **Citation removed 16 Sep 2026.** An earlier draft cited Ryan Flaherty's claim that trap bar relative strength predicts the 40 and the vertical "with 99% accuracy," plus his list of four "speed-indicative" exercises. That is a practitioner assertion, not a published finding, and the meta-analytic evidence contradicts the split-squat half of it for sprinting. The trap bar choice stands on the mechanics above. See `EVIDENCE-AUDIT.md`.

> **Unilateral work reframed.** The rear foot elevated split squat and single leg RDL are **change-of-direction transfer work, not sprint work**. Unilateral training showed no significant pooled effect on 10, 20 or 30 m sprints; effects appeared only for the 5 m sprint and one COD direction, small in magnitude. They stay because cutting is his priority.

> **Push press cut 16 Sep 2026**, replaced by the single-arm landmine press. The collision-sport evidence supporting upper body work is about *horizontal* strength: bench 3RM is associated with tackling ability, and higher upper-body horizontal strength with selection and reduced tackling injury risk. None of it is about pressing overhead, and a pole never does.

### MONDAY — full body, max force emphasis

| Exercise | Wk 1-3 | Wk 5-7 | Wk 9-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trap bar deadlift | 4x5 @ 70-75% | 5x3 @ 82-87% | 3x3 @ 85% |
| Rear foot elevated split squat | 3x6/side | 3x5/side loaded | 3x5/side |
| Bench press | 4x5 | 5x3 | 4x3 |
| Chin-up | 4 x max (add load above 10) | 4x6 weighted | 4x5 weighted |
| **Nordic hamstring curl** | 2x4 | 3x6 | 3x8 |
| Seated calf raise (soleus) | 3x12 | 3x12 | 3x10 heavier |
| Pallof press | 3x10/side | 3x10/side | 3x10/side |

**Nordic hamstring curl is the most evidence-backed exercise in this document.** Injury prevention programmes including it reduce hamstring strain injury risk with a risk ratio of 0.51, roughly a 50% reduction. The mechanism is fascicle length: a biceps femoris long head fascicle shorter than 10.56 cm carries about four times the future injury risk, and every 0.5 cm gained cuts risk by around 74%. Eccentric only, lower as slowly as he can control, push back up with the hands. He will be extremely sore for the first two weeks. That is expected and it resolves.

### WEDNESDAY — full body, velocity and power emphasis

| Exercise | Wk 1-3 | Wk 5-7 | Wk 9-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trap bar jump | 5x3 @ 20% BM | 5x3 @ 30% BM | 6x3 @ 20% BM |
| Single leg RDL | 3x8/side | 3x6/side | 3x6/side |
| Skater bound (frontal plane) | 3x5/side | 3x5/side | 4x5/side |
| **Single-arm landmine press** | 4x5/side | 5x3/side | 4x3/side |
| Chest supported row | 4x8 | 4x8 | 4x6 |
| **Copenhagen adduction** | 2x6/side | 3x10/side | 3x15/side |
| Tibialis raise | 3x15 | 3x15 | 3x20 |
| Side plank with hip abduction | 3x30s/side | 3x40s/side | 3x45s/side |

**Trap bar jump loading.** Peak power in the hex bar jump squat holds up at 20% load and remains near unloaded levels even at 40%, which is an advantage over the straight bar jump squat where unloaded generally optimises power. 20 to 30% of body mass is the working range: roughly 45 to 70 lb for him.

**Copenhagen adduction** follows the validated progression: two sessions per week building from 2x6 per side to 3x15 per side across eight weeks. Low eccentric hip adduction strength is a primary groin injury risk factor and groin injuries are endemic to the change of direction volume this plan prescribes.

### Isometrics: the underrated block for a tall athlete

Add on low days, weeks 1 through 8, three times per week: **4 sets of 20 second holds at roughly 70% of maximal effort** in a split squat isometric and a wall-supported calf isometric.

Six weeks of isometric training produced a **61.6% increase in tendon stiffness versus 29.4% for plyometric training**, with rate of force development up 16.7% and jump height up 64.3%. For a 6'5" athlete with long Achilles and patellar tendons who has to absorb 226 lb of momentum on every break down, tendon stiffness is the quality that lets him stop without collapsing into the joint. Deceleration specific isometrics also acutely improve change of direction performance.

Build stiffness first with isometrics and eccentrics, then learn to use it with high amplitude plyometrics. Not the other way round.

| **3-cone drill** | The single best separator of D1 lacrosse starters from non-starters | **Under 7.25 s** |
| **5-10-5 pro agility** | Short area change of direction, the core defensive skill | **Under 4.45 s** |

| **Vertical jump (CMJ)** | Separated D1 starters; college lacrosse benchmark is 29" | **30"+** |
| **Trap bar deadlift 3RM** | Best strength expression for a 6'5" frame, strongest link to speed | **2.0x BW = 455 lb** |

**Be clear on what is sourced and what is not.** The vertical jump benchmark (29" for college lacrosse), the strength multiples (college football Level 4 is 1.75-2.0x BW squat and 2.0-2.25x BW deadlift, elite above that) and the 300 yard shuttle population average of 52 to 53 seconds are from published sources. **The 3-cone, 5-10-5 and 10 yard targets are my extrapolation for a 226 lb athlete, not published lacrosse norms**, because position-and-bodyweight specific norms for D1 lacrosse defensemen are not publicly available. Treat them as a stretch standard, not a cutoff. A 6'5" 226 lb kid running 4.45 in the shuttle is a genuinely rare athlete; the sub-4.2 numbers circulating online belong to 5'10" defensive backs and are not the comparison.

**Do not chase body weight.** He is already 35 lb above the D1 defenseman average. Added mass from here is added momentum to brake, on longer levers, into a knee. If body weight rises above 232 while the 5-10-5 does not improve, that is a failing plan regardless of what the bench does.

## PART 6: THE PART THAT ACTUALLY DECIDES THIS

The training plan is necessary and it is not sufficient. Contact opened 9 September 2026. Coaches evaluate through the club circuit first, then film, then references from club and high school coaches. Run this in parallel starting this week or the physical work has no audience.

1. **Film, within 14 days.** Three to four minutes of game footage, not a highlight reel of big hits. For defense specifically coaches look for communication, successful stick checks, and smart quick decisions with the ball after a takeaway. Include full possessions and defensive recovery, not just the clips where he wins. Coaches consistently prioritise athletes who make smart decisions and compete hard over athletes with occasional highlight plays.
2. **Lead with the measurable.** 6'5" and 226 at close defense is the first line of the email, because it is the thing that makes a coach open the film. It is a genuine outlier and it should be stated plainly, with the date it was measured.
3. **Outreach is his to send, not yours.** Coaches read for whether the player wrote it. Short, specific to the programme, film link, club team and schedule, academics, and one line on what he is working on physically.
4. **Club schedule for summer 2027 is the real decision.** That circuit is what this 12 week block and the winter block after it are training for. Where he plays next summer matters more than any single set in this document.
5. **Re-test 1-5 December and update the film and the numbers.** A defenseman who can show a coach a measured improvement between September and December is showing coachability, which is a separate and valuable signal from the numbers themselves.

## APPENDIX: THE ONE THING THAT BEATS EVERYTHING IN THIS DOCUMENT

Sleep duration under 8 hours is associated with **1.7 times greater injury risk** in adolescent athletes. Adolescent athletes average roughly 6.3 hours against a recommendation of 8 to 10.

He is a junior, in a recruiting year, adding three hard training days to an existing schedule. If he sleeps 6.5 hours, the plan is a liability rather than an asset. Nine hours is the target. It is free, it is the highest return item here, and it is the one most likely to be skipped.
