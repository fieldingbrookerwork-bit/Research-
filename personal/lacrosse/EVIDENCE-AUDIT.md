# Evidence audit — every drill in the plan

Run 16 September 2026, after the 3-point start was found to have entered the plan from
the football source base rather than from anything lacrosse-specific. This audit asks the
same question of all 40 items: **is this here because evidence pointed to it, or because a
source I read happened to use it?**

Tiers:

- **A — Direct evidence.** A dose-response finding, a controlled trial, or a lacrosse-specific
  result. The prescription itself (sets, load, frequency) traces to data.
- **B — Sound mechanism.** Not lacrosse-specific, but the mechanism is established and the
  transfer argument holds.
- **C — Convention.** In the plan because it is standard practice in the sources I read.
  Defensible, not earned. Transfer evidence is thin or contested.
- **D — Cut.** The evidence does not support it for this athlete in this position.

## TIER A — direct evidence (9 items, all kept unchanged)

| Item | What actually supports it |
|---|---|
| Nordic hamstring curl | Injury-prevention programmes including it cut hamstring strain risk, RR 0.51. Fascicle length mechanism: <10.56 cm biceps femoris long head carries ~4x risk, +0.5 cm cuts risk ~74%. Hamstring tear is the third most reported NCAA men's lacrosse injury. Dosing taken from the trial protocols. |
| Copenhagen adduction | Adductor Strengthening Programme cluster-randomised controlled trial. The 2x/week, 2x6 to 3x15 progression over 8 weeks is the trial protocol, not an invention. |
| Flying sprints / max velocity dose | U-shaped injury curve: lowest incidence at 5-11 max-velocity reps/week, 6-10 exposures above 95% optimal. Eccentric hamstring strength fell at 7-8 weekly efforts above 90%. The 6-8 cap is that finding. |
| Heavy sled load (69-96% BM) | Cross et al., optimal load for peak power in sled-resisted sprinting sits near a 50% velocity decrement. Directly contradicts the conventional 7-20% BM guidance, which is why the number looks high. |
| Trap bar jump load (20-30% BM) | Published optimal loading range for peak power in the hex bar jump squat; power holds up at 20% and remains near unloaded levels at 40%, unlike the straight-bar jump squat. |
| Isometrics (split squat, wall calf) | Six weeks of isometric training produced 61.6% tendon stiffness gain vs 29.4% for plyometric, RFD +16.7%. Deceleration-specific isometrics acutely improve COD performance. |
| 40 yd shuttle, 6 reps / 25 s rest | The validated repeated shuttle sprint protocol from the field sport literature (20+20 m with a 180° turn, 25 s passive recovery). Not a number I chose. |
| The Friday braking block, as a category | Lacrosse GPS: defenders post the greatest number of decelerations of any position. Up to 70% of non-contact ACL injuries occur in poor deceleration. Harper's braking performance framework. |
| Seated calf raise (soleus) | The soleus is the main decelerating force at midstance and contributes more than half the vertical support of the centre of mass during running. Seated, knee bent, so it loads soleus not gastroc. |

## TIER B — sound mechanism (13 items)

| Item | Basis | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Wickets | Constraint-led and self-correcting: overstride and you knock them over. Specifically right for a tall athlete whose failure mode *is* overstriding. Practitioner method, not trial-evidenced. | Keep |
| Falling start | Teaching tool for forward shin angle, costs nothing, no stance skill required. | Keep |
| Trap bar deadlift | Bilateral training is what provides the force foundation for sprinting and jumping per the unilateral-vs-bilateral meta-analysis. The trap-over-back-squat choice rests on lever-length mechanics at 6'5". | Keep. **Citation dropped**, see below. |
| **Bench press** | **Upgraded on re-examination.** 3RM bench press is strongly associated with tackling ability; players with higher upper-body horizontal strength are more likely to be selected and show reduced injury risk when tackling. Close defense is a collision role. | Keep, rationale strengthened |
| Break-down start / backpedal to sprint | "Pair drills with sport-specific patterns like defenders backpedalling then sprinting" is the named recommendation in the transfer literature. | Keep (added 16 Sep) |
| Skater bound | Frontal-plane power and single-leg landing control. Landing control *is* braking, and defenders lead the field in decelerations. | Keep |
| Single leg RDL | Hamstring strength through length, plus balance demand. | Keep, **reframed** |
| Rear foot elevated split squat | Unilateral transfers to multi-planar cutting and single-leg tasks. | Keep, **reframed** |
| Chin-up, chest supported row | Pulling volume for postural balance against the pressing, and for stick work and contact. | Keep |
| Tibialis raise | Ankle sprain is the second most reported NCAA men's lacrosse injury. Mechanism reasonable, evidence thinner than Nordic or Copenhagen. | Keep |
| Pogo hops | Ankle and tendon stiffness prep before sprinting. | Keep |
| Extensive tempo at 70% | Francis method. Aerobic adaptation and CNS recovery without competing with the high days. | Keep |
| Lacrosse technical drills (zig-zag approach, chop into drop step, drop step and recover, lateral shuffle to sprint, reactive 1v1 mirror) | Coaching convention rather than trial evidence, but drawn from lacrosse coaching sources. It is the sport's own technical vocabulary, so specificity is maximal by construction. | Keep |

### The two reframes

**Rear foot elevated split squat and single leg RDL are not speed exercises.** The
unilateral-versus-bilateral meta-analysis found **no significant pooled effect of unilateral
training on 10 m, 20 m or 30 m sprints.** Significant effects appeared only for the 5 m
sprint and for change of direction to one side, and the magnitudes were small. The stated
conclusion is that bilateral training provides the force foundation for sprinting and
jumping, while unilateral training transfers that capacity to single-leg jumps and
multi-planar cutting.

They stay in the plan, because cutting is his priority. But the rationale was wrong and is
now corrected: **they are change-of-direction transfer work, not sprint work.**

### The citation I should not have used

I cited Ryan Flaherty's claim that trap bar deadlift relative strength predicts 40-yard dash
and vertical jump "with 99% accuracy," and his list of four exercises "indicative of speed"
(trap bar deadlift, rear foot elevated split squat, chin-ups, sled pushes).

That is a practitioner assertion, not a published finding, and the meta-analytic evidence
above **contradicts** the split-squat half of it for sprinting. The trap bar decision still
stands on lever-length mechanics and on bilateral force being the sprint driver. The
Flaherty claim is removed.

## TIER C — convention, thin or contested transfer (4 items)

| Item | The honest position | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Pallof press | Core stability research links trunk strength to rotational power, but there is explicit controversy over whether those improvements translate into athletic performance, and an acknowledged lack of research on the relationship to sport-specific performance. The mechanism for a pole holding position against a leaning dodger is plausible and mine, not published. | Keep. Costs 6 minutes. **First thing to cut if a session runs long.** |
| Side plank with hip abduction | Same category. Lateral hip strength for holding a slide. Cheap, plausible, unevidenced. | Keep |
| Downhill overspeed | Rhea's Indiana result (+3 mph average top speed in a year, 6 players over 21 mph rising to 34) is a **programme-level outcome with many confounds**, not a controlled trial of overspeed sprinting. Already capped at 5-10% versus their full 5/10/15/20% progression. | Keep with the cap, honestly labelled |
| A-skip | **Changed.** Skip drills are better suited as part of a dynamic warm-up than as sprint-specific training tools; athletes take far fewer steps in the drill than in sprinting, and there is not enough evidence for them relative to other methods. Separately, **B-skip emerged as the strongest predictor of both 5 m and 20 m sprint performance** in a validated running-drill test battery. | **A-skip replaced with B-skip.** Reframed as warm-up rehearsal, not speed training. |

## TIER D — cut (1 item)

### Push press / overhead press — removed

Same failure mode as the 3-point start. It was in the plan because "full body lift" convention
includes an overhead push, not because anything pointed to it.

The collision-sport evidence that justifies upper body work is specifically about
**horizontal** strength: bench press 3RM associated with tackling ability, upper-body
horizontal strength associated with selection and with reduced injury risk in tackling.
None of it is about pressing overhead. A close defenseman never presses anything overhead;
his stick work and his contact are horizontal and rotational.

**Replaced with: single-arm landmine press.** It is an angled horizontal press, so it matches
the evidence, it is inherently anti-rotational so it loads the trunk function the Pallof press
trains unloaded, and the angled path is far kinder to a 6'5" athlete's shoulders than a
vertical press. Same slot, same sets and reps, per side.

## Summary

| Tier | Count | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| A — direct evidence | 9 | Unchanged |
| B — sound mechanism | 13 | Unchanged, 2 rationales corrected, 1 citation removed |
| C — convention | 4 | 1 changed (A-skip to B-skip), 3 kept and honestly labelled |
| D — cut | 1 | Push press removed, replaced with single-arm landmine press |

**What the two errors had in common.** The 3-point start and the push press both entered
through the same door: a source base that was football-heavy by request, carrying positional
conventions that do not transfer to a lacrosse pole. The training *principles* in those
sources transfer well. The *drills* carry assumptions about the athlete's position that have
to be stripped out.

**What I got wrong in the other direction.** I flagged the bench press as possibly weak
before checking. It is better supported than anything else in the upper body block, because
close defense is a collision role and upper-body horizontal strength has a documented
relationship to tackling performance, selection, and injury risk in contact.
